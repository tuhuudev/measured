Queues, measured: why latency explodes near full load

A server at 50% load and the same server at 90% load are not "a bit busier". Requests wait several times longer, and the slowest ones wait far longer still. Here is a real server measured against the textbook formulas.

One worker, random arrival times, service times averaging 10 ms. In a second run: 100 ms and 568 ms. Nothing about the server changed between the two cards, only how often requests arrived.

Nothing is broken at 90%. There is still 10% idle time. But requests arrive at random, so sometimes several arrive together and have to wait in line, and the closer the server is to fully busy, the longer it takes to work through such a pile-up. This article measures that, checks it against queueing theory, and shows why the last few percent of utilisation are the most expensive.

The setup

The lab runs a real TCP server in its own process. It has one worker and serves requests in arrival order. Each request carries how long it should take, and the server busy-waits exactly that long, so service times are precise. A client sends requests at random times (a Poisson process, the "M" in the formulas below) at a chosen load, and times each one from send to reply.

Two kinds of work, both averaging 10 ms per request:

  • Variable: service times drawn from an exponential distribution. Most requests are quick, a few are long. That's the M/M/1 queue.[1]
  • Constant: every request takes exactly 10 ms. That's M/D/1.[1]

Load (utilisation, ρ) is the fraction of time the worker is busy: arrival rate × average service time.[1] At each target load from 10% to 95%, the client sent 3,000 requests and the lab dropped the first 300 as warm-up.

Measured: time per request as load grows

For M/M/1, the textbook mean time in the system is T = 1/(μ − λ), which is the same as S / (1 − ρ) for an average service time S.[1] Plug in S = 10 ms. At 50% load, 20 ms: half of it waiting. At 80%, 50 ms. At 90%, 100 ms. At 95%, 200 ms. From 80% on, every halving of the idle time doubles the time per request. The measurements follow that curve: 21.6 ms at 50%, 50.2 ms at 80%, 127 ms at 90%.

The 90% figure came out above the textbook's 100 ms, and the 95% step, at 196 ms, isn't the clean doubling of the 90% one. Two reasons, both measured. First, the real load drifted: the 95% step actually ran at 98.6% because of the random draws. Second, near full load, 3,000 requests isn't enough to average out luck. More on that below.

Variable work waits twice as long

The general formula for one server with random arrivals, Pollaczek–Khinchine, says the average wait in line is λE[X²] / (2(1 − ρ)). It depends on the second moment of the service time, so on its spread, not just its average. With constant service the wait is half that of exponential service at the same load.[1]

Same average work, same load, more than twice the time per request. In real services, variable work is the norm: a cache miss, a slow query or a large payload are long requests that everyone behind them waits for. Making work more uniform, or moving the slow cases to a separate queue, cuts waiting even when the average doesn't change.

How many requests are inside

Little's law says the average number of requests in a system equals arrival rate × average time in the system: N = λT.[1] The lab counted requests in the server directly. At 50% load there were 1.12 on average. At 90% there were 12.0. It matched λT to three decimals at every load, as it must over a run that starts and ends empty.

That's useful in reverse: if you know your request rate and your latency, you know how many requests are in flight. Tracking that number, or the queue length, alongside utilisation shows how close a service is to its limit.

Is the real server the textbook server?

To separate the server's own cost from chance, the lab also replays each run through an ideal server: the same send times and the same service times, but no overhead at all. Up to 80% load, the real server's mean was within 0.9–2.2% of the ideal one. At 90% it was 2.8–5.4% slower, and at 95% 4.3–12.1%. The real server overran each 10 ms request by at most 0.12 ms (p99), about 1%. Near full load, even that 1% is amplified, for the same reason the waiting is.

Short tests at high load lie

Near full load, a queue has a long memory. One unlucky burst builds a backlog that takes a long time to drain, so neighbouring requests' waits are strongly correlated. In the lab's second run, variable service at a 95% target ran at 97.7% and averaged 501 ms, against 196 ms in the run shown above. The simulation below makes the point cleanly. At 90% load, its 200,000 requests averaged 102 ms (theory: 100). Split into windows of 3,000 requests, the windows ranged from 56 to 438 ms.

A five-minute load test at 90% can therefore look fine, or terrible, by luck. Test at high load for long enough, repeat it, and look at the spread, not one number.

Retries make it worse

Every retry is one more arrival. In the retries article, 200 clients sent between 901 and 1,512 requests to get 200 served, depending on the retry policy. Under load, retries raise ρ exactly when the server has the least headroom, which pushes everyone further up the curve. Backoff with jitter and a cap on retries keep that from turning into a spiral.

Rules of thumb

  1. Leave headroom. With random arrivals, waiting grows like ρ / (1 − ρ): 1× at 50%, 4× at 80%, 9× at 90%, 19× at 95%. Plan capacity so normal peaks sit well below the knee.
  2. Watch utilisation and queue length, not just averages. Average latency looks fine long after p99 has blown up.
  3. Reduce variability. Constant work waited half as long as variable work with the same average. Split slow requests into their own queue, cap request sizes, cache the slow cases.
  4. Test high load long enough, more than once. At 90% load, 3,000-request windows of the same simulation differed by about 8×.
  5. Don't let retries add load at the worst moment. Back off with jitter, and cap retries.
